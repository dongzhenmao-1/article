Prologue
---
This is not a story of devoting one's entire life to another

No matter where the road of life leads, it can only be my own path.

No matter what choice I make, no matter how wonderful they seem, or no matter how heavily those burdens cling to me — they are all things I have gained in the course of living, and they belong to me.

Therefore, as long as my little sister has a happy smile on her face, I can usually let most things slide with "whatever". This is probably the path I am destined to take.

And, it would take a long time for me to uncover this.

This almost casual attitude will determine me during my entire life.

But even if I discover it, the world has already been built, and there is no way to make any changes.

So, we have no choice but to keep living in this way.

This book is that kind of story.

0 ~ 15
---

I was three years old the year my little sister was born. I still remember that day, and I doubt I will ever forget her birthday, given that she was born on February 14th.

Record-breaking heavy snow fell in the days surrounding her birth, but the day she came into the world was clear and sunny all day. While the adults were bustling about, preoccupied with the thick snow piling up on the roads and rooftops, my only worry was whether the snow on the kindergartenplay ground would melt. Would I still be able to have snowball fight with my friends if it did? I rolled a snowball with my bare hands, lost in thought. My mind was so entirely consumed by snow that even when I got home from school and heard that my sister had been born, all I could manage was a flat "Oh," not caring in the least.

At that time, I could hardly understand what the word "sister" meant. Even when my parents told me that we were related by blood, I couldn't grasp the concept actually. Although I was told that, just like me, she had come from mom's belly, I didn't understand because I had no memory of being in there myself.

Another person was coming to live in our house, so my room would get smaller sooner or later.

Back then, this was all that I could understand.

I didn't go to the hospital to visit my newborn sister either, because my parents believed they shouldn't let a noisy kid like me go to the hospital to cause trouble. Six days later, mom brought her home from the hospital. It wasn't until then that I finally saw my sister for the first time. Since I saw my father was busy making the bed and preparing everything needed the day before my mother came home, I knew that my sister was finally on her way to our house.

And so, I had my first encounter with my sister, who was cradled in mom's arms. My first impression of her was "frail". She was tiny — even smaller than babies left at the nursery, and she had an M-shaped hairline and flushed red cheeks. She seemed as frail as a newborn puppy; and as if fully aware of her own helplessness, she would cry for help from anyone nearby.

To put it bluntly, I didn't feel like we belonged to the same species.

All in all, my first impression of my little sister was not very good. Terrified that a simple touch would make a terrible mistake, I soon began to consciously avoid her. A few years later, although still unsteady on her feet, my sister could finally stand by herself, and became "a creature more like me". But by then, the wall between us had already been built. Even if it wasn't thick, like a styrofoam wall which would crumble by a slight touch, it could completely obstruct our view, keeping us out of each other's sight.

About her at that age, the only thing I knew was she was very sensitive to cold.

Maybe because she was born on a snowless day — when she was about three years old, our father took us sledding in a winter of heavy snowfall. We had barely started playing when my sister burst into tears, crying, "It's too cold, I want to go home". Though I kept it to myself at that moment, I thought, "What a wimpy girl!". Mind you, at that time, I never imagined this opinion of her would remain unchanged in the years to come.

I had no idea how to interact with the creature who was a whole size smaller than me, and she was much closer to our parents than she was to me — crying out for them the moment she ran into trouble. Therefore, there was little room for me to step in, and I could barely say a word to her. But except when letting her guard down, she barely expressed her thoughts, and her personality certainly played a part in this as well. That was what I always thought. But since we had so little communication, I couldn't be sure if that was actually the case.

Initially, our parents planned for my sister to share the kids' room with me, but my sister was clingy to our parents — especially mom — so she ended up sleeping with them instead. I was delighted that my room remained mine alone, and I hoped it could stay that way forever. I believed that both of us would have felt suffocated, and our relationship would have grown worse if we had shared the same room.

At that time, neither of us probably realized we were siblings. Siblings should help each other. Even if this wasn't a written rule, at least, my parents hoped so. I could feel their expectation, but I deliberately pretended not to notice. As for my little sister, I doubted she understood a thing. After all, she was still too busy just trying to live, with no energy to spare for anything else.

In this way, we grew up without any building any relationships.

When my sister started to cry out for me, she was 6 years old, and I was 10 years old.

It was the end of August back then, the tail end of summer vacation. The sun started to set slightly earlier, but compared to that, what truly depressed me was the fact that school was about to start. Even though time was like water in a swimming pool, how had it all evaporated before I even realized it? Surely, the end of summer vacation had to be a lie, right? Yet, when looking down at my arms, I saw my skin bore the traces of the sun, proving otherwise. So all I could do was sigh over the undeniable evidence.

Just when I was in that frame of mind, sitting in front of the fan and scratching mosquito bites, I found a faint presence behind ne. I turned around — and saw my little sister standing there. Although I didn't say a word, I was so shocked that I forgot the itchy feeling. 

My little sister, who seldom approached me on her own, stood there holding a picture diary. "Please help me...", she whispered. The moment our eyes met, she timidly handed it out to me. Then, a sense of foreboding arose within me — which became a stark reality after I opened it casually.

There was almost no record of this summer in the book. "Wow—" I stroked the snow-white pages, exclaming in amazement.

Forget the text and images, even the dates were completely blank. I looked at my sister over the book, who was gazing at me with her watery eyes. Physically speaking, she was standing while I was sitting, so she was looking down at me. Yet for some reason, I had an illusion of looking down at her, and this might be the first time I had realized that my sister was so much smaller than me.

"Summer homework?"

I asked, and my little sister nodded softly. 

I remembered I had written the same thing when I was her age. With homework like a picture diary, you would definitely get scolded if you didn't finish it, no matter what excuses you came up with — and it wasn't practical for her to count on our parents. I finnaly realized the reason why she turned to me for help.

Except for the first three days, the diary was completely blank, which gave me the true understanding of "giving up after three days." I scratched my head in frustration; while I realized why my sister cry to me for help, but there was absolutely nothing I could do. I could barely remember how I spent my summer vacation, so of course, I had absolutely no idea about how she spent hers.

"What have you been doing all summer?!"

I didn't mean to blame her; I asked purely out of curiosity about how she spent her summer vacation. Had she been absorbed in some things that she was unwilling to make time to write in her diary? That was actually what I wanted to ask. But to my little sister, it sounded like a scolding. Tears began to swirl in her eyes. 

"Oh no!" I panicked; this was getting bad. My little sister was on the verge of tears, and cold sweat began to run down my back. I didn't want my mom to hear her crying and scold me, so I quickly pushed my little sister, who was about to cry, out of the living room. Although the fan was still running, I had no spare energy to turn off it.
